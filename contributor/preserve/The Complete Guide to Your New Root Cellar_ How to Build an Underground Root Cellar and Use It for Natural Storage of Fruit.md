Back to Our Roots Root cellars date back to the earliest days of civilization; archaeologists found evidence of ancient root cellars in Australia from 40,000 years ago. Of course, root cellars from this time consisted of little more than a hole dug into the ground to store yams. Our current vision of the constructed, walk-in root cellar filled with produce was most likely developed in 17th century England because people of this time had the perfect conditions for growing root crops and the ideal climate for preserving these crops underground. As these men and women immigrated to North America, they brought with them their knowledge of root cellaring. When they reached their new home, they found new food crops but similar geographies and climates — and native populations willing to share their methods of preservation. Learning from these practices, the immigrants modified their root cellars to work in their new land and passed these ideas on to the next generations. As settlers moved west, so did this type of root cellar and the preservation techniques to go with it. During even the harshest Northern Plains winters, pioneer families survived off the produce and other food they packed away in their cellars. Created before full basements existed, these spaces doubled as storm shelters, safe houses, and even hiding places for slaves on the run. Built to last, thousands of these structures still stand today in the open or hidden under the grasses of original homesteads. Colleges and archaeologists throughout North America are now searching for these lost root cellars and documenting any they find. The National Register of Historic Places keeps a list of discovered root cellars, and after they are registered, they will be chronicled and preserved for future generations. With the advent of electricity, refrigeration, and industrialization, the practice of root cellaring fell out of favor. As people moved to towns, they had no place to put root cellars and began to rely on buying produce from corner stands and supermarkets. That is an impossible task. No matter how many high-tech gauges or state-of-the-art storage systems you use, you cannot control the elements. There will be potatoes that sprout or apples that go bad. You will walk in one winter day to find too much humidity, and you might even have trouble with mice finding your treasure trove. Your goal as you build and maintain your new cellar is to keep things within a range, and your produce will never be the wiser if something goes wrong. Besides, by living a root cellar lifestyle, you have a little extra to spare. The perfect location For best results, place your root cellar in a location that optimizes the earth’s cooling power. Picking the perfect spot for your root cellar depends greatly on whether you are starting from scratch. If so, you will have a little more control over the big issues, such as orientation and ground cover. Whether you are digging a hole or refitting your basement, the two big concerns with location are access and site placement. Obviously, for root cellars in the home, this is not such a big issue. However, with both types of root cellars, also consider access within the cellar, such as shelves that are at a height at which you can see and reach everything. Site placement Site placement involves harnessing elements above and below the ground and putting them to work for you. In nearly all regions, the best placement for a basement or excavated root cellar is in a northern or northeastern site. These sites will receive the least amount of light and solar heat and will maintain the most consistent temperatures. In cold regions, you might want to consider a site with more southern exposure because you might need that winter sun to keep up temperatures within the cellar. For basement construction, the best choice is a corner room because two walls will be underground and provide those beneficial earth-cooling temperatures. Consider drainage as you are mapping out a site. If you can, place your root cellar higher than the land around it so water runs past it. Before you pick a spot to completely dig out, look around your property for an existing hill or elevated rise that you could use with minimal excavation. In the end, site placement is not a deal breaker. So, pick a site that best approximates what you need, and you can make adjustments during construction to compensate for shortcomings. A constant, cold temperature inside the root cellar Because the goal is to store produce in an environment that slows the deterioration process, the key element of this environment is a year-round root cellar temperature just slightly above freezing. The temperature should not be so cold that the produce freezes and breaks down but cold enough that it sits in limbo. A root cellar and the produce stored within are happiest at a temperature between 32 and 40 degrees. Again, this is a range and can be shifted upward depending on the type of produce you are storing, and for short-term storage of a month or two, a range of 40 to 50 degrees will do just fine. Some produce, such as pumpkins, eggplant, or garlic, even prefer a higher temperature. See Appendix A for a Produce Storage Chart that lists the optimum storage conditions for each produce type. In most regions, the ground below the frost line stays at or near 52 degrees, and changes in air temperature or solar heat do not constantly affect it. Traditional root cellars are built below ground so it is easy to maintain a constant temperature without many additions, such as heaters or circulating fans. Once you have placed your storage space in this precooled, below-ground area, whether it be a hole dug in the ground or in your existing basement, you will be able to borrow this cold energy and harness it to maintain your root cellar. How to measure temperature An accurate measurement of your root cellar’s temperature is critical to successful storage of all your produce. Do not take chances by guessing the temperature by feel. The temperature inside your root cellar will not directly correlate to the outside temperature. You do not need fancy or expensive equipment to accomplish this task. A simple outdoor thermometer is perfectly suited to monitor your root cellar’s temperature. Available at any home improvement center, your thermometer should have a good maximum and minimum read so you will be able to track the variations during the day without having to visit your root cellar every few hours. Once you get accustomed to your cellar’s normal temperature, you will be able to quickly notice changes and make adjustments. This information will also come in handy when you are working on your journal and developing your maintenance plan for the following year. For larger storage areas, consider using two separate thermometers: one placed near the ground and one placed near the ceiling. There will be a difference in temperature between low areas and high areas, and knowing this number will help you select the proper storage positions for your produce. This is exactly what can happen in your root cellar, and this dry air will draw the moisture out of your produce. Without enough humidity, your produce will quickly shrivel and decay. Too much humidity will also adversely speed up the maturation process and can even damage the structures, such as wood supports, you have built. Humidity levels also alter the temperatures within your root cellar. Most of the items you will store in your root cellar will require humidity between 70 and 90 percent. How to control your temperature and humidity You develop many factors controlling the temperature and humidity of your root cellar at the beginning stages of construction. Site placement, insulation, construction methods, and vent placement all affect how well your root cellar is able to maintain optimal levels. Design plans included in Appendix B offer ideas for vent placement, and later chapters will offer seasonal ideas for adjusting and controlling these indoor factors. Breathing room, or ventilation Every root cellar needs a way to breathe. Fresh air needs to get inside the cellar to circulate, and old air needs a way to get out. As produce matures, it can release ethylene gas and odors. These gasses will then negatively affect the maturation rate of other nearby produce or cause unpleasant flavors. Have you heard the old saying “one rotten apple can spoil the whole barrel?" This saying is referring to ethylene gas. With good ventilation and proper shelf spacing, the air will easily move through your space to help eliminate these gasses and odors. Ventilation is also a key component in controlling heat and humidity. Hot air rises, and cold air falls. This principle is essential to properly achieving ventilation. Just think of your root cellar as a big layer cake of air. The coldest, most humid air is on the bottom, and the warmest, driest air is on the top. You want to work with and adjust these layers so the conditions within your root cellar are right for your specific produce. By increasing or decreasing the airflow, you can rearrange these layers. The best root cellar designs include at least two vents with one placed low as an intake and one placed high as an outlet. In larger spaces, the door can also serve as a venting mechanism. With proper venting, you will be able to let cold night air in when you need to quickly lower the temperature or let air out during warmer days if you need to lower humidity. Like everything else, this step takes a little practice to master, but once you have worked with your root cellar for about a year, you will be a pro at operating your ventilation system. Drainage and weatherproofing Drainage and weatherproofing are part of the site planning and new construction phases, but they do require upfront explanation. When you think of drainage, consider that you are essentially digging a big hole in the ground. Water flows downhill and loves to collect at the lowest point possible. The site you choose might not now have any issues with storm runoff, but once you dig that hole, the water might like the new path you have created. And water sitting in or around the walls of a root cellar can spell disaster — for your structure itself and the produce held within. Review where the water runs around your property during storms, where snow collects during winter, and where snow eventually melts. Consider taking preventative measures, such as tiling or rerouting downspouts, to direct the water away from your structure. The type of soil in and around your root cellar also affects drainage. The best soil is sandy and gravelly and lets the water run right through; the worst type is a thick clay soil that holds the water on top of the surface for long periods. This assessment especially is important if you are planning a dirt floor in your root cellar, which is the best design. This will be discussed more in Chapter 4. Once you have determined your soil type, you could make minor amendments during construction if needed to bring it to a better standard. Weatherproofing also relates to the conditions from outside sources that can affect the inside of your root cellar. For example, adequate insulation in colder regions will help the root cellar maintain a more steady temperature and humidity during hard cold spells. The various options in these areas will be discussed throughout this book and within the designs. Pest and rodent control Any space filled with food that is accessible to the outdoors is going to attract rodents. The mice, chipmunks, and other critters that have been happy living in your woodpile all summer would much rather relocate to your well-stocked root cellar for the winter. Guarding against rodents is simple when you address it during construction and look after it routine maintenance. The primary control method is to keep them out in the first place. Look at every opening into your root cellar and think like a mouse. Vent edges, doorways, drains, and even the walls can be access points for a tiny mouse. Take these prime prevention measures: Screen vents and install weatherstripping on your doors. Keep the door shut whenever possible unless you can monitor it. Insulate with spray foam around vent, light, and door edges. Check wood exteriors for rot, which is easy to chew through. For an extra measure of protection on a wooden door, install a piece of sheet metal along the bottom edges; this cannot be chewed through. Clean up any food debris that falls near the door of the root cellar. The smell can attract rodents. Set traps within the root cellar in case a rodent sneaks in unnoticed. Using poison is not recommended in food storage areas. Insect control is less of an issue within root cellars because the conditions are too cold for insects to live in. However, you can transport in the occasional worm, ant, or bug when you are bringing in your produce. Once inside, they might do minimal damage to the produce you have stored. Now you know the mechanics of running a root cellar, you are probably wondering what exactly you can store within this amazing structure. And the answer is, with a little creativity, just about anything. Some vegetables, such as potatoes, parsnips, and carrots, are perfectly made to live in a root cellar without a care for months. Others, such as melons or leafy greens, can survive, but they need more love and attention. It really depends on the amount of work you want to put in and the types of meals you would like to prepare throughout your storage season. The types of items you can possibly store in your root cellar break down into general categories of easy-to-keep with a high success rate and finicky keepers with unreliable results. They are listed here in order of easy to difficult. The easy items can go directly from the garden to storage with a quick dusting and a good inspection for insects and quality. The more difficult keepers will require special, advanced preparations or extra attention during storage, but it also can be fun and adventurous to try out something new. Later chapters will cover these items in more detail when it comes to storage, and Appendix A includes a detailed chart listing storage requirements by produce type. The following breakdown will help you choose the type of produce best suited to your needs: Easy and reliable. These are the primary winter root crops and staples of the root cellar. They are fairly easy to grow and perfectly suited to the conditions of your root cellar. They are loaded with nutrients but sturdy enough to stand up to cold storage. These crops include: veggies that grow underground, such as potatoes, carrots, rutabagas, beets, and parsnips; produce that has meaty fruit, such as cranberries, apples, pears, and kohlrabi; and hearty greens that are members of the cabbage family, such as brussels sprouts, cabbage, and kale. Most of these also ripen late in the season, so you can leave them in the ground until your root cellar is cooled down and ready for company. These items also include juices or ciders made from fall produce. Fair but needs special attention. These fall into a secondary category of crops that ripen near the end of summer or in the fall but do not like the same conditions as the easy crops. Produce in this category, such as citrus, garlic, and squashes, all store perfectly well in a root cellar that has higher temperatures and lower humidity, which might require more work on your part. Homemade beer, wines, and ales also fall into this category. Picky but possible. Many root cellar users have good luck with this group of produce, but some see only dismal failure. It takes some practice and experimentation to succeed with these items. Produce here includes tomatoes, cucumbers, melons, eggplants, and berries. Many gardeners use their root cellars to hold these end-of-season, picky vegetables for a few weeks so they can spread out their canning and freezing workload. Other produce that falls into this category, such as Chinese cabbage, endive, celery, and rhubarb, can be fooled into growing when placed in this dark, damp environment. Other foods. This is the catch-all category that will help you round out your family menu. Depending on the conditions of your root cellar and the amount of segregating you want to tackle, you can store just about anything. Dried beans, nuts, butter, cheese, eggs, and cured meats all do well with proper storage conditions. Miscellaneous. Your root cellar is not necessarily just for food. Store flower bulbs, saved seeds, and perennials. Some annuals can overwinter if kept cool and in the dark. Your root cellar can be used to force indoor plants into blooming. And do not overlook this space for storing emergency supplies, such as extra water, first aid kits, and batteries. Just be sure to keep them in an airtight container. Items not suited for storage. In a traditional root cellar, the humidity level is too high to adequately store many items moisture could damage. This is not the place to store canned goods, either store-bought or those you have canned. The moisture will quickly eat away at any metal parts and potentially expose your canned goods to bacteria and spoilage. Whole grains, such as oatmeal; coffee or tea; dried herbs; flour; and sugar are all really good at absorbing moisture and odors, and unless you can keep them in a well-sealed container, they will not last in these conditions. Drainage Tiles It is impossible to choose a site with perfect drainage, and once you have introduced a new hole to your topography, it will attract flowing water. You might encounter unexpected drainage problems. Water draining in or near the root cellar is a problem because water might seep into the structure. Water pooling, freezing, and exerting pressure against your walls can cause the walls to fail and collapse. The only way to avoid this possibility is to install drainage tile around the footings and halfway up the wall if you have extra drainage concerns. This is a simple and inexpensive step that will give you added insurance, and putting drainage tiles in at the beginning is easier than trying to retrofit your cellar when you find drainage issues. TIP! Drainage tiles are a bit of a misnomer because they are not tiles. They are continuous, flexible plastic pipes used to channel unwanted water in a certain direction, such as away from structures or growing areas. Lower drainage tiles are best placed after the footings are cured but before the walls are built. The best system is to use 4-inch diameter perforated plastic walls are built. The best system is to use 4-inch diameter perforated plastic drainage tiles wrapped with a sock to prevent soil from infiltrating the tile line. You can find prewrapped drainage tiles at farm supply stores, landscaping shops, or home improvement centers. Before placing the tile, pour a 2-inch layer of gravel around the perimeter of your footings. Slope the gravel slightly toward the front of your structure. Lay the tile on the gravel so the top of the drainage tile is just below the top of the footing. If you use more than one piece of drainage tile, connect and seal each section according to the manufacturer’s instructions and finish with the open ends about 1 foot past the front corners of your cellar. If you are creating a separate entrance, make sure the tile ends past this point. As you place the drainage tiles, make sure no dirt gets inside the line, or it will become clogged and no longer work. At the end of the tiles, dig a hole about 3 feet wide by 3 feet deep and fill it with gravel. Place the open end of the line in the center of each filled hole. Often referred to as a “French drain,” this extra step is not required but is effective for areas with poor drainage. After installing the line, cover it completely with about 12 inches of crushed stone or gravel to help the water seep through to the drainage line and help support the weight of the backfill. While working with the drainage tile, be careful not to step on it or crush the line. Preparing the soil Your garden soil is the food for everything you grow, and getting this soil in tiptop shape before you plant is essential. If you are planting a new garden in high-quality soil, you do not have to worry about testing soil acidity or adding amendments. Most vegetable plants require a pH of 6 to 6.5. A good helping of compost or commercial fertilizer will return your soil to the proper level. In some cases, you might have to add lime to counteract acidity or organic matter to adjust alkalinity. Refer to the instructions included with your test kit, or ask for suggestions at your garden center. TIP! Later sections will discuss using hydrated lime to adjust root cellar humidity levels. You can also use this lime in your garden. Once it becomes saturated from your cellar, sprinkle it on your garden to help with acidity and boost calcium. Another spring job for the gardener is to turn the soil. This dirty job involves getting knee-deep in your garden and churning the compacted and clumpy soil from last year. People with large gardens use a rototiller for this job because it is fast, easy, and efficient at breaking up those big chunks. Tillers can be rented at any local hardware store, but this job can absolutely be tackled with only a potato fork, a shovel, and a strong back. Your ultimate goal is to get your garden soil to a loose consistency and free of any materials or debris that will impair the seeds’ growth. Try to avoid walking over the area you have tilled because this will compact the soil and make it hard for the seeds to take root. Tilling is also the perfect time to add compost, manure, or other amendments, such as lime. After your first tilling, add in the amendments and run the tiller through once more. Now, your garden is ready for planting. Feed your garden some compost Compost is basically decomposed plant material, such as spoiled produce or food scraps, yard debris, grass clippings, or even coffee grounds, and it makes a fantastic organic addition to your garden. Many home gardeners keep a compost heap as a way to recycle garden waste into soil for next year’s garden. Now is a good time to start your own compost pile. Composting is more complicated than just throwing your waste in a corner of the yard, but once you get it going, it will become second nature to your gardening. The most important aspects to learn about composting involve which types of waste to add, when to aerate it, and how to tell whether it is ready for use. These are all simple skills to learn and are covered in most gardening books. A website with thorough information and instructions for starting your own compost is the University of Minnesota’s Extension site at www.extension.umn.edu/distribution/horticulture/DG3296.html. If your community has a leaf and yard waste dump, they might also offer free compost to residents. Ask at your local government offices. Adding compost is as simple as spreading a thin layer over the top of your garden, or you could till it in when you turn the soil each spring. For deficient soils, spread about 1 inch it in when you turn the soil each spring. For deficient soils, spread about 1 inch of compost on top of your entire garden, and then add one-quarter to one-half inch every year. The following is an easy formula for determining how much you will need to cover your garden: Square feet of the garden surface area x 144 = your garden’s cubic inches. One 5-gallon pail contains about 1,150 cubic inches. For example: a 100-square foot garden would need 14,400 cubic inches of compost. This is about 13 pails of compost for a 1-inch application, six pails for an application of one-half inch, and three pails for one-quarter inch. Stopping weeds from the start A well-turned and well-fed garden will nurture your plants to perfection but also attract weeds from all corners. An unavoidable partner in every garden, weeds are unattractive and will compete with your plants for water, sun, and nutrients. Weed seeds get into your garden from a variety of sources: bird droppings, barnyard manure, straw used for mulch, mature weeds that drop seed heads, and even the breeze. Most of these sources of seeds are out of your control, so you have to be particularly vigilant about removing weeds at the first sign of growth. Garden plants are most vulnerable in their beginning months, and weeds will quickly choke them or stifle their growth. Some gardeners apply a treatment of herbicides as soon as they turn the soil, but this adds toxic chemicals to your soil and defeats any hopes of organic produce. Others lay a sheet of black plastic over the freshly turned soil as a way to heat and kill the weed seeds, but this only works for the short term, and the plastic can deprive your soil of water and oxygen. The best type of weed barrier is a breathable, biodegradable covering that blocks weed growth but allows water to penetrate. If you browsed the aisles of your garden store, you would see numerous types of landscaping fabric or geotextiles. These are perfect for perennial beds you are not replanting every year or in areas in which you can cover the fabric with decorative mulch. In a veggie garden, though, you do not want to commit to certain plant placement year after year, and geotextile fabric is expensive when you have to buy enough for a large garden. One of the best weed barriers you can choose is probably right in your recycling bin: old newspaper. Newspaper has many advantages as a weed barrier: It is inexpensive and readily available, it breaks down quickly, and it will help aerate your soil. Newspaper is also an effective groundcover to prevent water from splashing on the plants’ leaves, which can lead to disease. Here is how to create a newspaper weed barrier in your garden: 1. Take a typical section of newspaper of about five to six pages. Make sure to use the black and white sections, not those with colored ink. 2. Keep each section folded, and dip it in a bucket of water until it is saturated but not crumbling. 3. Carefully place the wet newspaper between the rows of newly planted seeds or seedlings. Be sure to overlap the edges as you put the sections down. For larger plants, you can also lay the wet paper down and poke holes through it for the plants. 4. Optionally mulch over the newspaper with a thin layer of soil or organic mulch, such as bark or grass clippings. Avoid using straw or hay because these often have weed seeds. This step is not needed, but it does cover the unattractive newspaper. 5. Leave in place all summer, and till in whatever remains after the fall harvest. Keep your seedlings consistently watered; use the fine mister to keep the soil moist but not soggy. Your seedlings have delicate roots and will not survive overly wet or dry soil. If seeds are sprouting too close together, carefully pull the seedlings out of the tray so you leave just one close together, carefully pull the seedlings out of the tray so you leave just one seedling per pot or one per inch. This will give the plants plenty of room to grow and develop healthy root systems. As the seedlings get bigger, continue to thin or transplant them as needed. Installing the Ceiling Depending on your structure, you might not even need a ceiling. For example, a structure with a concrete roof does not need additional ceiling materials other than for minor insulation or appearance. In some cases, a ceiling will adversely affect the storage conditions in the cellar. Hotter air naturally rises, so a ceiling might trap that warm air around the produce you stored on the top shelves. Conversely, a ceiling that traps warm air might help you when maintaining a temperature above freezing is hard. If you are unsure, install a removable ceiling or one you can remove in sections. After the first year of operation, you will know whether the ceiling helps you, and you can choose to make it a permanent feature. If you decide to go with a permanent ceiling, install a “sandwich” of materials to provide good insulation, especially if your root cellar is underneath your home. The sandwich consist of a vapor barrier as the farthest layer from the cellar, a piece of 2-inch thick rigid foam insulation, and a piece of plywood as the ceiling of your cellar. These can be assembled with construction glue and attached to the ceiling joists when ready. Interior Walls Interior walls fall into the same category as ceilings — not essential but nice to have for extra insulation and beauty. For concrete construction, wallboard is unnecessary, might inhibit temperature and humidity control, and adds an extra step with more cost and maintenance. For timber construction or retrofits of existing spaces, you will build walls with standard stud frame construction. The one difference with this project is you will have to take extra precautions for moisture that could come up from the basement floor. Installing Control Mechanisms After the shelving goes in, it is time to add the bells and whistles. Most of these items are not essential but make running your root cellar easier and less stressful. Requirements for many of these areas have been discussed in previous chapters. You do not have to install all these things to be able to use your root cellar. You can always later upgrade your systems. Thermometer and hygrometer The first and most important control mechanisms to install are the thermometer and hygrometer, or a combo unit. By putting these in early, you will have time to learn how to use and adjust them, and you will be able to start accurately monitoring the conditions within your root cellar before your produce moves in and these measurements become essential. Refer to the manufacturer’s instructions for installing and operating the instruments. You might want to place two separate units: one near the ceiling and one near the floor. Conditions will vary from floor to ceiling, and these multiple placements will give you more accurate readings. Start to cross-reference these levels with the outdoor temperatures and humidity levels. Be sure to include weather events, such as rainfall. What’s Going On in the Garden? You have probably been making daily trips to your garden and seeing how quickly everything has started to grow. Your planted seeds should all be springing up by now, and your seedlings should be well-established and growing. If you find any gaps where plants should be growing, you might have had a seed failure. You have time to replant, but be sure to buy new seeds and mark the new row with this information. You must continue to feed and water your garden, but make sure to not overdo it. The soil should stay consistently but just barely moist, and the summer rainfall might be enough. Plants will grow strong root systems if they have to work a little to reach water, and a stronger root system prevents a plant from easily falling over from wind or heavy fruit. Vining plants, such as cucumbers, squashes, beans, and peas, should be ready to attach to a trellis or vertical support. This will train them to grow upward and prevent them from taking over your garden with their long tendrils. As soon as the plant has about 4 inches of growth, begin directing it toward the trellis. As the vine or stalk grows, gently tie a piece of nylon stocking or soft yarn around the vine to your trellis or stakes, and make sure the stalk of the plant is not rubbing against the stake. Do not cinch the tie because this might damage the stalk and inhibit further growth. Continue to tie new growth. You can also place tomato cages or bamboo supports around your tomato and pepper plants to support the heavy fruit they will soon bear. Critter and pest control Your plants are now at a critical stage because the new growth is tasty to the animals living in your area. Deer and rabbits love newly sprouted beans and peas, raccoons and woodchucks cannot wait to dig up tasty roots, and squirrels and gophers like to raucously play in the garden. Previous chapters covered fencing and folk remedies. Effective organic sprays are available to deter these critters from raiding your garden. One visit could destroy your entire crop. Pest control is a separate issue. Each variety of plant will attract a different pest, and each pest requires a different control method. Some can be removed by hand while others must be deterred with powders and sprays. Do some research at your local library or online to learn more about which sort of pest control methods will work for the specific varieties you are planting. Not all insects are bad. Many insects and worms improve your garden soil and help pollinate your plants. A garden full of strong plants and healthy soil will attract beneficial insects, and a struggling, unhealthy garden will be a bonanza for the bad bugs. Take care of your garden, and you will not have to worry too much about insects. Weeds, weeds, weeds A healthy garden is also a good weed deterrent. If you have kept ahead of the weeds, your garden plants are now becoming the main occupants of their areas, and weeds will not find room or nutrients to grow. It is important to help these young plants establish their territory, so keep a lookout for any weeds, pull them as soon as possible, and use a small garden hoe to gently work the soil in between and around your rows of veggies. You can also apply another layer of saturated newspaper or mulch. This technique is discussed in Chapter 4. Planting a second crop As summer moves on, some of the early crops, such as spinach and snap peas, will finish producing, and you can plant a second crop in that space. Once the plant stops producing or dies back, remove the plant material and hoe the area in which the plants were growing. Be careful around the other plants growing in your garden so you do not disturb their roots or leaves as you are working. Plant your next crop, and mark the row with the seed information. You could also practice staggered planting, a method of gardening in which you plant the same seeds but plant a row every two or three weeks. This will stagger the harvest time and spread out your end-of-season workload over a more manageable period. Staggered planting also protects your crops from massfailure events, such as unexpected frost, infestations, or disease, because the crop will be at two or three different stages of development and not susceptible to the same dangers. Finding Alternate Sources for Produce You do not have to be a gardener to keep a root cellar. Most cities now boast high-quality farmers markets, food cooperatives, and farm alternatives from which consumers can purchase a wide array of fresh produce. Buying this produce is often less expensive than growing your own and is cheaper than buying supermarket produce. These local farm-to-table suppliers are assets to the root cellar owner and sources of information about the types of produce grown in your area. These sources could also be used as a supplement if your crops failed or did not produce as much as you wanted. It is too soon to begin storing produce in your root cellar, but early summer is the best time to research your options for outside suppliers. This will give you ample time to establish relationships with your favorite farmers and bump you to the top of their list when harvest time rolls around. Farmers markets are a treasure-trove of fresh foods and friendly smiles. Visit each week, and you will be sure to find something pretty, interesting, and tasty for your table. The produce you find at farmers markets is often grown organically and picked the day of the sale. However, local suppliers are not always certified organic producers. This certification requires numerous steps over a number of years, and they might not have gone to the trouble of becoming official organic growers. They might practice organic farming, employ safe environmental practices, and avoid using pesticides. Ask them questions when you meet with them because most farmers love talking about their business. To find suppliers in your area, watch your local paper, ask your neighbors, or check your city's website. Two websites on which to search for local suppliers around the country are www.localharvest.org and http://attra.ncat.org, the site for the National Sustainable Agriculture Information Service. This site also has gardening resources. Both of these sites offer a search by location so you can find suppliers by area or by the type of produce you are looking for. The three most common supplier types are farmers markets, food cooperatives, and community supported agriculture suppliers. These are each discussed in the following sections. Also consider looking at www.craigslist.org, your local classifieds, or bulletin board posts. Farmers markets Farmers markets are the most informal suppliers but often those with the most variety and lowest prices. They often spring up once per week in a central location, such as a park, and local growers come and go with a wide range of products. You can find just about anything, from eggs to pie to fresh produce, at the farmers market, and these markets often run throughout the winter. You can glean valuable information about growing produce in your climate. Most growers will be happy to offer advice and help you choose varieties. The wide range of produce also gives you a good chance to sample different varieties without committing to growing a whole row in your garden. Visit often, and get to know the suppliers. Take the names and phone numbers of those folks from which you would like to buy more quantities. Be sure to practice good farmers market etiquette, and do not buy out their entire stock. Instead, order large quantities ahead of time so you save the farmer the hassle of hauling all that produce to market; they might even be willing to deliver it to your doorstep. By ordering ahead, you will probably get the pick of the crop without having to be first in line, and the produce will be fresher and less damaged because it has been handled less. Food cooperatives A food cooperative is run the same way as a small grocery store, except it is stocked with organic, unprocessed foods and grains and locally supplied items. Shoppers become members by paying membership fees and are expected to help with stocking, selling, and other store activities. This membership buys a specified amount of food each month at a reduced rate and might give members first choice of new items coming into the store. In some cases, you might be able to shop at the co-op without being a member, but you will have to pay a higher cost per item. These are not the best places to find large quantities of produce for a root cellar. You can get information, try new varieties, and connect to larger produce suppliers. Community supported agriculture Community-supported Agriculture (CSA) works similarly to a food co-op but is farm-based with mostly fresh produce offerings. A local grower offers shares in his crops, and for each share, you will receive a basket of produce each week during the growing season. The produce will be whatever the farmer has picked that week. In some cases, shareholders are allowed to go to the farm and pick produce themselves or specify the type of produce they want. Most CSAs have a pickup day each week, and you can either go directly to the farm or pick up your produce at a central location. Closely inspect produce for insects or pests. The conditions in a root cellar are not conducive to insect survival, but insects will be happy to eat their way out if they get stuck inside a storage bin. Remove any pests you find, and make sure the insect has not damaged the produce — any bites or burrows will spoil veggies. As you place the produce in its storage container, handle it carefully. Do not toss or drop into bins or barrels. Each bruise will lead to decay, so place each item by hand, and you will be much happier with the outcome. Most veggies and fruit can go right into the cellar without any further preparations, but some do require curing to get them ready for storage. The area nearest the ceiling has the highest temperature and lowest humidity, and the areas near the floor and intake vent have the coolest temperature and the highest humidity. The spaces in between the ceiling and floor show a gradual transition between the two zones. In a large root cellar, this creates a range of prime storage conditions you can use for a variety of produce. For example, beets prefer 32 degrees and a moist 95 percent humidity while fennel bulb prefers a warmer 35 degrees with 90 percent humidity. So, you would store the beets closer to the floor and the fennel on a middle shelf. As you place produce in your root cellar, try to place it in the zone best suited for its specific needs. Storing Items other than Produce in the Root Cellar A cool, moist, dark root cellar is perfect for produce and, with a little extra time and attention, can also be used for other food items, garden plants, and even emergency supplies. Some dried goods, such as sugar, flour, grains, coffee, and dried herbs, will not do well in a root cellar. They will absorb moisture and quickly go bad. If you must store them in the cellar, put them inside a sealed plastic bag and then inside a sealed plastic tub, and place them in the location nearest to the ceiling. The same advice applies to canned goods. Anything with a metal lid or ring will quickly spoil in this moist environment. Leave these items in your regular pantry. Some untraditional items do well in the root cellar; it just takes some practice and experimentation to see what works best. Check the many links and books listed in the Resource section in Appendix C for more information on making and storing these items. Wine Most wine drinkers know this is a picky beverage that needs consistent storage conditions to age well. Most wines prefer a storage temperature range of 50 to 55 degrees and humidity levels at about 70 percent. Wine can tolerate colder temperatures and higher humidity but will take longer to mature. The levels needed for wine are not optimal for produce, so you might want to consider storing your wine for the long term in a different room. In addition, some of the more odoriferous items stored in a root cellar, such as cabbage or onion, can create an off taste in wine. Ciders, ales, and juices Ciders, ales, and juices are more forgiving than wine and can be stored temporarily in the root cellar. Store-bought, shelf-stable beverages that are sealed well can be stored in the root cellar with no problem as long as they do not have metal lids. Homemade, nonfermented fruit and vegetable juices must be handled differently because they do not contain the preservatives needed to handled differently because they do not contain the preservatives needed to make them shelf stable without refrigeration. Depending on the type of beverage you are trying to store, your root cellar might not provide the proper storage temperatures to keep it from spoiling. Refer to your recipe for proper storage temperature, and only keep juice in the root cellar if you know you can consistently maintain its required temperature. With a root cellar lifestyle, you no longer have to make huge batches of juice. Now, you can store the raw ingredients and make the fresh juice as you need it. Cured meats Cured meats, such as ham, bacon, and smoked fish or meat, are also items that could do well in a root cellar if you take extreme care monitoring their conditions, especially temperature. These meats must be kept at 40 degrees or cooler to maintain their freshness. The odors of smoked meats can affect some of the tastes of your produce and, conversely, strong odors from onions or cabbage can change the taste of your stored meats. Take care when storing these items separately. Dairy and eggs Hard cheeses, butter, and yogurts store well in a root cellar with a consistent temperature of 40 degrees or lower. One day of higher temperatures can cause nearly instant spoilage. However, these items must also be kept unfrozen because this will also change their tastes and textures. The root cellar can also be a place to keep fresh eggs as long as they have not been washed. Freshly laid eggs are protected with a coating called bloom, and once this is removed, the shell becomes susceptible to bacteria infiltration. With the bloom intact, eggs can be kept for up to four months in covered containers. Storage conditions for eggs must be temperatures between 33 and 40 degrees and humidity at 70 percent. Before storing, place eggs in cold water and discard any that float to the top because these might have hairline cracks that will cause spoilage. Dried beans, nuts, and seeds From dried kidney beans to walnuts to sunflower seeds, pantry staples are a From dried kidney beans to walnuts to sunflower seeds, pantry staples are a fibrous and protein-rich addition to any diet. They do take up a lot of room in the pantry, and you might be tempted to store them in your root cellar. Adequate storage conditions are difficult to find in a traditional root cellar, but beans and seeds can be stored with a little extra care. For the best results, keep each item separately in a tightly sealed container in the driest section of your root cellar. Be sure beans and seeds are completely dry before placing them in storage; a small amount of moisture will cause the entire container to rot. If possible, leave nuts in the shell until you are ready to eat them. Shells will provide an extra layer of protection from the elements. Plants and bulbs If you keep flower gardens, the end of the season means the end of those pretty plants. But with the aid of your root cellar, you might be able to overwinter some of the plants you throw away every fall. Hardier annual plants, such as geraniums, will survive the winter if placed inside your root cellar before a killing frost. To save these plants, cut the foliage back, and dig the plant up while leaving the roots intact. Place the plant in a black plastic bag, and put it in the warmest section of your root cellar. Plants can also be left in their pots if you have grown them in containers all season. Just cut back their foliage, do not water, and cover with a bag in your root cellar. When early spring comes, remove the plant from the bag and look for buds or sprouts. If you see any signs of growth, plant in a small pot, water, and place in a sunny window or underneath grow lights. If the plant starts to show green, it will survive, and you can plant it in your outdoor beds as soon as it warms up. You might be surprised by how many annuals you can grow from year to year with this approach, and even with a low success rate you will save a money. Refer to your favorite gardening book for more information on and tricks for overwintering annuals. TIP! You can also save indoor flowering plants, such as poinsettias, from year to year by using this same bagging method. Flowers that grow from bulbs or tubers, such as gladiolus, canna lily and iris, can also be stored in the root cellar from year to year. Just dig up the bulb after the leaves have died back, gently separate new bulbs that are growing, leave them to dry in the sun for a few days, and place them in a mesh bag. Hang the bag from a rafter in your root cellar, and replant in the spring. Tulips, daffodils, hyacinth, and other bulbs that flower in the spring can be left in the ground because they can survive the freezing winter temperatures. You can force bulbs to grow indoors, too. Saving seeds for next year’s garden Saving seeds for next year’s garden Many gardeners use their end-of-season plants as supply for next year’s garden. The seed-saving procedure differs between plants. It is best to refer to your favorite gardening website or book for more information on the best way to save seeds from your specific type of plant. One suggestion, no matter which type of seed you are saving, is to gather seeds from a variety classified as heirloom or open pollinated and not a hybrid or F1 variety of plant. Hybrids are created from crossing parent plants to select for one specific plant trait, such as shape or color of produce. Subsequent seeds from these mature plants can be sterile or might grow produce with different traits from the parent plant. Heirloom plants will consistently produce fruit true to the parent. Be sure the seeds are dry before storing them, and refer to the previous section for information on how to store seeds in your root cellar. TIP! It is not necessary to have all of these tools in your toolbox because many of them will be used only once. Many of the larger items can be rented by the day from do-it-yourself centers. For worksite safety, be sure any borrowed, rented, or previously owned tools work properly and are up to safety codes. Carpentry tools Chalk line: Used to create a straight line connecting two points. Framing square: An L-shaped tool used for determining right angles. Types include: Steel square: Used to keep things perpendicular. It is also handy for measuring. Combination Square: A device with a 6-inch steel tool used to mark 45and 90-degree angles. Hammers: Claw: The most common type used in construction. The head should be made of steel. The face is used to drive nails, and the other end has a twopronged claw used to remove nails. The handle can be made of fiberglass, wood, or steel. A good all-purpose claw hammer weighs about 16 ounces and has a 16-inch handle. The claw hammer is best for finer carpentry work versus demolition work. Framing hammer (heavy-duty): Comes with steel heads that weigh 20 to 32 ounces or titanium heads that weigh 12 to 16 ounces. They have a wooden handle with a straight claw. Also used for masonry work. Mallet: This tool is made with either a rubber or wood head. It is used for soft impact drives and will not damage the surface. It is often used with chisels. Levels: Used to make sure a wall, floor, or any flat surface is level. They come in different sizes: 30-inch square level with three glass bubbles filled with liquid so various surfaces can be made level, and a small multi-function laser level to make a straight line between two points. Planes: Used to smooth the surface of woodwork by paring shavings from it. Block plane: A small, low-angle plane with the blade at a low pitch used to finish off rough edges and cut across the grain of wood. Jack plane: Used to smooth rough boards and flatten uneven surfaces. Pliers: Tin snips, linesman’s, vise-grip, Channel Lock® or slip-joint and side cutters. Plumb bob: Used to find a point directly beneath another point. For instance, it can be used when building a flight of stairs. Saws: There are several sizes, styles, and types of saws available. Each has its own specific purpose. The following are the key ones needed for this project: Hand saw (panel saw): Known for its portability, ease of use and efficiency; this is the most common handsaw. Its blade is about 2 feet long and tapers from the handle heel to its toe. Rip saw: Cuts in the direction of the grain. Crosscut saw: Cuts across the grain. Both are ideal for cutting large, wide, or thick pieces of wood. Use an 8-inch for rough work and 10-inch for finish work. Keyhole saw: A saw that typically has a 6-inch narrow blade used to cut drywall, plastic, cement board, and soft woods. Sawhorses: Good for creating flat work surfaces. Screwdrivers: Regular flathead: One for large screws and one for small screws. Phillips head: One for large screws and one for small screws. Multi-head screwdriver Steel tape measure: 12-foot to 16-foot in length. Stepladder: A short folding ladder with a small platform. A 6-foot high ladder will reach high places and is best to have as a small, handy platform to place paint, tools, or supplies. Utility knife: A handheld razor blade in an adjustable holder. Buy an extra package of blades to keep on hand. Ladders: 8-foot or extension ladder. Also handy for temporary access while excavating. Heavy-duty construction or digging tools Backhoe: Piece of excavating equipment with a bucket attached to a hinged pole on a long arm. Used to dig holes and remove soil. Small versions can sometimes be rented from do-it-yourself centers. Hacksaw: small, handheld saw with fine teeth used to cut metal. Landscaper’s paint: Spray paint that works upside down. Use to mark the digging area. Mallet: Hammer with a head made out of wood, the softness of which will not scar the object being struck. Post-hole digger: Tool with two long handles and curved bottom blades designed to fit into post-holes to scoop out the soil. Sometimes available as an attachment to gas-powered equipment. Pry bars or crowbars: Made from forged steel. Used to pry things apart or loosen big rocks. Types to have on hand: Gooseneck wrecking bar: More heavy-duty with a flat end for pulling nails. Cat’s paw: A small crowbar used in small, tight spaces. Shovel: Scoop-style is ideal for picking up piles of debris. Usually made out of aluminum with a wooden handle. Sledgehammer: A large, heavy hammer with a head made out of forged steel and a long wooden handle. Used for breaking up chunks of rock. Wear safety glasses when using this tool. Skid steer: Also called bucket loader or skid steer loader. A small rigid frame, engine-powered machine with hydraulic lift arms designed to accept interchangeable attachments such as a bucket, a fork arm, or an auger. Comes in a range of sizes and might either be four-wheel drive or on skids. Sometimes referred to by one of its brand names “Bobcat.” Power tools Circular saw: A power saw with a circular blade sticking out through a flat base. Ideal for making straight and fast cuts on woods of different thickness. Compound miter saw or chop saw: Used to make straight cuts but is similar to a miter box in that it can make 45-degree and 90-degree cuts by rotating the tool’s turntable. Cordless drill: Used the same as a corded drill but is powered from a rechargeable battery. Easier and safer to use when work on ladders or damp ground but does not have the same amount of heavy-duty power a corded drill has. Electric corded drill (3/8-inch size): Drills holes and drives screws, but with additional attachments, it can sand, grind, and stir paint. Extension cords: Use only heavy-duty cords designed for outdoor use and with high-powered equipment. Radial arm saw: A circular saw rail-mounted to a pivoted arm. Reciprocating saw: A saw that cuts through metal, wood, or plastics and is well-suited for demolition jobs, such as removing walls or creating rough openings for plumbing. Rotary hammer and hammer drill: Used for boring through cement and foundation walls. Table saw: A saw on a table used to rip long planks of wood. Chainsaw: Useful for trimming trees or buried roots in the way of construction. Gardening tools Hand trowel: Handheld pointed-or round-tipped shovel used for digging small holes for transplanting seedlings. Hoe: Comes in a variety of shapes. Choose a solid triangular shaped head, which is good for marking rows and weeding between rows. Hose and spray nozzle: Choose a heavy-duty rubber hose long enough to reach your garden. Select a spray head with multiple settings from fine mist to stream. Light gardener’s twine: Used for trellising vines and marking planting rows. Pitchfork or potato fork: Long-handled, long-tined tool used for loosening soil or digging root crops. Rake: Choose a heavy-duty metal rake with widely spaced tines for leveling tilled ground. Rototiller: A gas-powered or hand-pushed tool with sharp blades designed to be pushed through soil to break up chunks. Available in many sizes and usually rentable by the day through retail outlets. Rubber kneeling pad: Protecting knees for working in the garden. Shovel: Comes in different sizes and lengths. It is good to have one with a long handle and a pointed and sharp spade. It is important to keep the edge sharp. Watering can: Choose a light plastic can with a light coverage nozzle. Wheelbarrow: A handy one-wheeled cart used to haul materials. Finishing tools Center punch: This steel rod has a pointed end, and it is useful when making an indentation in a wooden or metal surface. This tool makes it easier to help start the hole before using an electric drill. Hole saw: A spade drill bit used to enlarge existing holes or drill new round holes usually about 1¼ inch in diameter. Masonry bits: Used to drill holes in concrete. Plumber’s bit kit: Includes different size bits to accommodate drilling the larger holes involved in plumbing projects. It is more affordable to buy bits in a kit than to buy them individually. Masonry tools Concrete finishing trowel: A finishing tool that smoothes and seals wet concrete. It is a flat piece of metal with a wooden handle that comes in different sizes to accommodate different projects. Mason’s hawk: A rectangular flat surface to hold mortar with a handle under the surface that is perpendicular. Mason’s string: Made out of a heavy string that does not stretch. Used as a guide in carpentry, masonry, and other construction projects. Mason’s trowel: A flat trowel that is triangular in shape and used when applying mortar to brick or stone. Miscellaneous tools Allen wrenches: Metal, L-shaped, six-sided tools that can drive screws and other fasteners that have recessed hexagonal heads. They come in a set with various sizes. Caulk gun (and various caulks based on intended use): The caulk gun holds the different tubes of caulk for ease of use. Flashlight: Handy for various reasons. Nylon line: Braided nylon rope is useful for tying down tarps or materials during transport. Stud finder: There are a few different styles of this tool, which is used to find the studs in a wall when it is covered with a wall covering, such as drywall. Plumbing tools Basin wrench: Used to install or remove the mounting or coupling nuts on faucets. Flange maker: Used to flare out the end of a pipe. Flux: A paste you put on pipes to clean them before soldering them. Pipe cutters: Used for cutting plastic PVC or metal pipes and is available in a variety of sizes. Plumber’s putty: Sealant used to help prevent leaks around drains and sinks. Plungers: Used to clear sinks and toilets. Silicone tape: Used to wrap around threaded fittings to seal them and help prevent leaks. Solder: A soft metal sold as a wire that is used to join pipes and fittings. Stillson wrench: Large, heavy-duty, adjustable wrench used for plumbing and demolition projects. Tongue and groove pliers: Comes in different sizes with wide and adjustable jaws used to grasp, bend, cut, and shape objects. Also called by the manufacturer’s name of Channellock® pliers. Tubing cutter: A handheld tool with a sharp cutting wheel used to cut soft metals, such as copper, aluminum, and brass. Wire brush: Used to clean metal pipes before soldering or joining together. Safety tools First-aid kit: Can be purchased with basic items to use in case of a minor accident or injury. For those who prefer to assemble their own kits, the American Red Cross suggests these basic items: Tweezers and scissors A blanket and an instant cold compress Individually wrapped assorted sizes and shapes of bandages, rolls of bandage, and sterile gauze pads Adhesive cloth tape Absorbent compress dressings Nonlatex gloves, antiseptic wipes, and antibacterial ointment Plastic breathing barrier with a one-way valve to perform CPR Hard hat: A construction hat made of hard plastic you use during demolition or other renovation projects during which debris could fly. Hearing protectors: The American National Standards Institute (ANSI) and the Acoustic Society of America has determined and approved, through guideline ANSI S3.19-1974, a specific style of disposable foam earplugs that mold to the shape of the ear canal or earmuffs. Buy a whole box, and place them in your ears when you use loud power tools or complete other loud tasks. Look for the ANSI code on the box. Nose and mouth filter mask: A protective mask that keeps out dust, sand, and pollutants. Safety goggles or glasses: These should meet ANSI guidelines. ANSI approved Z87.1-2003 are a replacement for Z87.1-1989 (R-1998) specifications; devices that meet the “High” impact criteria are marked “Z87+”. Work gloves: Choose heavy-duty gloves made of leather or cloth to protect your hands when you pick up debris; they also help to grip and grab objects. Table of Contents Glossary Aerate: Loosening compacted soil to allow better water penetration and root growth. Annual: A plant that must be replanted every year from seed. Backfill: Gravel or earth replaced in the space around a building wall after foundations are in place. Batt: Insulation in the form of a blanket, not loose filling. Bearing wall: A wall that supports a floor or roof of a building. Berm: A narrow shelf or flat area that breaks the contiguity of a slope and is often used to direct water away from a structure. Biennial: A plant that lives for two years and typically produces flowers and seed in its second year. Boiling water canner: A large, deep saucepan equipped with a lid and a rack to lift jars off direct heat. Used to can high-acid foods. Brassica: A group of plants from the mustard family, cruciferae, that includes Brussels sprouts, cabbage, kohlrabi, rutabaga. Brine: A high-salt solution used for pickling or preserving foods. Circuit breaker: A protective device that automatically opens, or trips, a circuit without damage to itself when the current exceeds a predetermined level. Compost: Organic matter created from decomposing plant materials. Culvert: A closed conduit used for the conveyance of surface drainage water under a roadway, railroad, canal, or other impediment. Drain tile: Pipe made of perforated plastic, burned clay, concrete, or similar material, laid to a designed grade and depth to collect and carry excess water from the soil. Erosion: The wearing away of the land surface by water, wind, ice, gravity, or human activity. Ethylene gas: A plant hormone released as a colorless, odorless gas from certain fruits and vegetables. Ethylene gas can accelerate the ripening process of some produce. Fermentation: Process of preserving food through a reaction caused by yeasts not destroyed during processing. Can cause illness if done improperly. Footings: Concrete base underneath the foundation designed to distribute the weight of the structure. Forcing: Creating artificial environmental conditions to force a plant to grow or bloom. Forms: The total system of support for freshly placed or partially cured concrete, including the mold or sheeting (form) that is in contact with the concrete. Foundation: Lower parts of walls on which a structure is built, typically made of concrete blocks or poured concrete. Freeze: When the surface air temperature is expected to be 32 degress or below over a widespread area for a climatologically significant period of time. Use of the term is usually restricted to occasions when wind or other conditions prevent frost. Frost: The formation of thin ice crystals on the ground or other surfaces. Frost develops under conditions similar to dew, except the temperature falls below 32 degrees. If a frost period is sufficiently severe to end the growing season, it is commonly referred to as a killing frost. GFCI (Ground fault circuit interrupter): A device that detects an insulation failure by comparing the amount of current flowing to electrical equipment with the amount of current returning from the equipment. Whenever the difference is greater than 5 milliamps, the GFCI trips and thereby interrupts the flow of electricity. Hardening off: Process by which plants grown indoors are slowly acclimated to outdoor conditions before transplanting. Heirloom plant: Plant that has been grown from the same seed line for many generations. Humidity: Amount of water vapor in the air. Hygrometer: Instrument that measures the relative air humidity. Joist: A small rectangular sectional member arranged parallel from wall to wall in a building or resting on beams or girders. They support a floor or the laths or furring strips of a ceiling. Lid: (As related to canning) a flat metal disk with a flanged edge lined with sealing compound. Used with a metal screw band for vacuum-sealing jars. Moisture barrier: Treated paper or metal that retards or bars water vapor, used to keep moisture from passing into walls or floors. Mulch: Loose organic material, such as bark or grass clippings, placed over soil to impede weed growth and hold in soil moisture. Perennial: Plants that grow back every year without reseeding or transplanting. Pesticide: A general name for agricultural chemicals used to control conditions that damage plants. Also referred to as herbicide (weed control), insecticide (insect control), fungicide (fungi control), nematocide (parasitic worm control), and rodenticide (rodent control). pH (potential of hydrogen): A numerical measure of hydrogen ion activity, the neutral point being 7. All pH values below 7 are acid, and all above 7 are alkaline. In relation to canning, foods are divided into low acid and high acid, which require different canning methods. Precast concrete: Concrete members, such as walls, panels, slabs, columns, and beams, that have been formed, cast, and cured before final placement in a structure. Pressure canner: A tall, heavy pot with a lid that is locked in place and a pressure-regulating device. The lid is fitted with a safety valve, a vent, and a pressure gauge. Used to process low-acid foods. Relative humidity: Refers to the amount of moisture in the air compared to the amount it could hold at a certain temperature. If air temperature reaches the dew point, the relative humidity is 100 percent. Spoilage: Evidence indicating the food is becoming unsafe to eat. Signs include mold, brown spots, slime, or odors. In canned foods, signs of spoilage include bubbles, cloudiness, seepage, and broken seals. Thinning: Removal of seedlings to allow neighboring seedlings room to grow. Can also refer to removing tree, bush, or plant branches to promote healthy growth of the plant. Tile drainage: Land drainage by a series of drain tile lines laid at a specified depth, grade, and spacing. Transplanting: Moving a plant to another site by digging it out and replanting it.
